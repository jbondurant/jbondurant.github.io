What card counting is worth at the Casino de Montréal's blackjack

Justin Bondurant-David · version draft, 2026-10-23

At the Casino de Montréal's blackjack, played with 8 decks, a player who counts without mistakes, bets from one unit up to 8 units and plays every decision by the count wins about 0.47% of the minimum bet per round with the published count that scored highest here (Victor Advanced Point Count + ace), and wins about 0.36% with Hi-Lo, the most common count, when the dealer puts the cut card 80% of the way into the shoe. With bets averaging 1.5 units, that is about 0.31% and 0.24% of the money bet. At 70%, the shallowest of the three places studied, Hi-Lo loses 0.03% and Victor Advanced Point Count + ace wins 0.04%. I have not measured where Montreal's dealers put the cut card. Someone who plays the standard strategy chart without counting loses 0.66% of the first bet of each round. At $25 a unit, that is about $12 per 100 rounds, against a swing of ±$610 over the same rounds.

Every decision is played from a table of index plays the program worked out for this game, not from the authors' published index lists; published estimates for a six-deck game say a short list captures less (see Prior work). "One to 8 units" means the same bet sizes for every count, averaging 1.5 units, handed out in the order of each count's own estimate of the advantage.

The question

I wanted to know which counting system is worth the most at a table I can actually play at, measured carefully enough that the differences between systems are bigger than the measurement's own uncertainty.

Card counting books rank their systems by correlations computed from small tables, or by simulations of games with other rules. Neither tells me what each count is worth at the casino in my own city, with its rules, its shoe and its cut card, under one bet schedule that treats every count the same.

The game

The rules are the Casino de Montréal's: 8 decks; the dealer hits on soft 17 and checks for blackjack; blackjack pays 3 to 2; you may split up to 4 hands, you may not hit split aces, and you may double after a split; surrender is not offered.

The rules sentence is printed from the rules object the program ran with, not written by hand, so this page cannot describe a game the code did not play.

How counting works

A count gives each card a small number, its tag. Adding up the tags of every card you have seen tells you roughly whether the cards still to come favour you or the dealer.

The running count is that sum. The true count is the running count divided by the decks left, so that the same surplus of small cards means more near the end of the shoe. A count is balanced when its tags add up to zero over a full deck. Its level is its largest tag. An index play is a departure from the chart when the count passes a number; a side count is a second count kept of one rank, here aces. Insurance is the side bet on the dealer's blackjack, worth taking only when the count says enough ten-valued cards are left. Every result is an expected value, always written as % of a unit per round.

The box under the answer gave six words a line each; here they are more fully. Penetration is how deep the cut card sits. The unit is the smallest bet, and the spread is how far the bet grows from it. "The same bet schedule" means every count gets the same bet sizes, handed out in the order of its own estimate of the advantage, so a count is credited only for how well it orders the rounds. The chart is basic strategy. Fitted plays are the index tables this program chose from the data. Points are percentage points of the minimum bet per round.

How it was computed

Instead of playing millions of hands and averaging what happened, the program took 60,743 moments from 60,000 shuffled shoes and worked out exactly what every possible first move was worth from each one.

A worked example, computed while this page was built: off the top of a fresh shoe, a hard 16 against the dealer's 10: standing is worth −0.5408 of the bet, hitting −0.5360.

The program chose each count's plays and bets from the data, picking on half of the shoes the move and the bet that did best at each count, then graded those choices on the other half. That is what "fitted" means here.

  1. A shoe, shuffled from a recorded seed.
  2. Dealt with the chart down to the cut card.
  3. A moment in that shoe: the cards left, called a state.
  4. Kept with chance q and weighted by the inverse of q, so the sample stays fair.
  5. The 550 possible deals from it (two player cards and the dealer's up card), each with its legal first moves.
  6. The exact value of each move over every way the round can finish, about 1,720 numbers per state.
  7. Each count's plays and bets fitted on half the shoes and scored on the other half.
  8. 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the shoes, the same ones for every count.

Why exact values, not a simulation

The best counts differ by a few hundredths of a percent. A simulation that plays two counts on the same rounds cancels most of the luck they share, but it still needs about 3,361,404,932 rounds to tell them apart as finely as this study does. Exact values remove what is left: the luck of the card order within each moment. What remains is the luck of which shoes came up, the same for every count.

First decisions and full play

Every count was scored twice: once using the count only for the bet, insurance and the first decision on the two cards dealt, and once for every decision in the round, including later cards and split hands. Across the published counts at 80% and one to 8 units, using the count everywhere adds between +0.022 and +0.097 points (percentage points of the minimum bet per round).

What a bootstrap is

The shoes are redrawn with replacement 1,000 times and every figure recomputed; the spread of those answers is the figure's uncertainty.

Results

The headline

At 80% and one to 8 units, the five published counts that scored highest here (Victor Advanced Point Count + ace, Hi-Opt II + ace, Revere 14 Count + ace, Uston Advanced Point Count + ace and Omega II + ace) are within +0.011 points of each other. This data cannot say which of them is best: it cannot separate Victor Advanced Point Count + ace and Hi-Opt II + ace; Hi-Opt II + ace and Revere 14 Count + ace; Revere 14 Count + ace and Uston Advanced Point Count + ace. It does rank Victor Advanced Point Count + ace ahead of Revere 14 Count + ace; Victor Advanced Point Count + ace ahead of Uston Advanced Point Count + ace; Victor Advanced Point Count + ace ahead of Omega II + ace; Hi-Opt II + ace ahead of Uston Advanced Point Count + ace; Hi-Opt II + ace ahead of Omega II + ace; Revere 14 Count + ace ahead of Omega II + ace; Uston Advanced Point Count + ace ahead of Omega II + ace. Each of the five beats Hi-Lo.

The pairs are not written by hand: every pairwise difference among the five is taken resample by resample, widened for the later plays held as fitted, and given one multiplier over all of them, so the pairs called separate are separate together, not one at a time.

Penetration

Where the dealer puts the cut card matters more than which count you use. Hi-Lo goes from −0.076 at 70% to +0.294 at 80% (first decisions, one to 8 units), a bigger change than the gap between Hi-Lo and the highest count.

Spread

With flat bets, even perfect knowledge of the first move loses 0.53% a round. The edge comes from betting more when the count is high.

Spreads past one to 8 units, and wonging (sitting out rounds when the count is low), are among the settings of the chart of every count above; with wonging on, its caption gives the share of rounds played and the average bet.

Is there a better count?

A computer search for the best count of this kind, run on a separate set of 60,000 shoes and then scored once here, found one that beats every published count I tested: F2 scores +0.409% of a unit a round at 80% penetration with a 1-8 spread, +0.022% more than the best published row, Victor Advanced Point Count + ace, and every published count by at least +0.020% (the smallest lower end of the 95% intervals). The gain is small next to the room left above it: perfect knowledge of the cards left would earn +0.522%. The count's tags are not meant for a person to keep; they show how much better a count of ten numbers can do at this game, not a system to play.

Flat-betting, the searched count F1 flat beats every published count I tested, by at least +0.002%.

The tags were searched on a second file of shoes from a different seed, so the shoes the count is graded on played no part in choosing it. Not yet computed: the searched counts' scores on the search file. Figures from the search file are labelled so every time they appear and are never used in a headline.

Betting and insurance correlations

QFIT gives the insurance correlation of 6 of the counts scored here, and this study's measured insurance correlation agrees with QFIT's for 6 of them.

Once this study's betting correlation is divided by R, the highest betting correlation any linear count reaches on the same states, it is within the check's tolerance of QFIT's for 4 of the 5 counts QFIT gives one for. The table under its chart gives each verdict, which says "agrees in direction" where QFIT's figure is for another game than this one.

The betting correlations that do not agree once divided by R, 1 of them, are in the table under their chart; the table of claims above gives the reason the check gives for each.

The two betting correlations measure different things. QFIT's correlates a count's tags with the effects of removal, how much taking one card of a rank out of the shoe moves the player's expected value. This study's correlates the count's true count with each sampled state's actual advantage under the chart, on eight-deck states. No count of one number per card follows that advantage exactly, so this study's correlation is not on QFIT's scale until it is divided by R. The insurance correlation needs no such step: both measure how well the count tracks the share of ten-valued cards left.

What a hash proves, and what it doesn't

A SHA-256 hash proves that a file is, byte for byte, the file it was taken from. The data files results.json and results.csv ship with this page, and SHA256SUMS lists their hashes, so you can check your copies (see Downloads). The page also lists hashes of the run files the figures were read from. Those files are not on this page, so you cannot check their hashes from it alone. That these are the files the figures were read from is my statement.

Running the code again would not settle the hashes of the saved results either. Each saved results file (the .ser files) records how long its own steps took, so a new run writes different bytes even when every figure is the same. What a new run can check is the figures. One count's fit was made again in a later run, on the same number of threads, and every figure came back to the bit; for the rest, that is a prediction. The states file should come back byte for byte if each of its segments is made again by the build that made it, but that has not been tried. For the first segment of the states file and for the search file, the build that made them is inferred, not recorded.

A hash says nothing about whether the code is right; a bug is hashed just as faithfully. The checks above are the evidence that the code is right, and they are evidence, not proof.

How AI was used

I started this repository in May 2022 with a Monte Carlo simulator I wrote myself. Since August 2026, most of the code, including the exact engine, the scorer, the count search and the second program, has been written by AI coding agents (Claude Code, from Anthropic) working from my questions and design decisions. I chose what to compute and read the designs and the results. AI agents ran the tests, looked up the published figures and compared them with mine. I did not write most of the Java myself. The reviews in How it was checked were also done by AI agents, from the same families of models, so they are less independent than a human expert's review would be, and no human expert has reviewed this work. The design notes and this page were drafted with the same tools and edited by me. Weigh the checks with all of that in mind.

Limits

These results are for one player alone at the table, counting without mistakes and seeing every card, at one casino's rules. They say nothing about being noticed, about other players at the table, or about other games.

  • The authors' own index numbers and true-count divisors are not used, so each row is the best those tags can do here.
  • The count does not move within a split hand, so full play is a lower bound.
  • The study rests on 60,743 valued states.
  • Full-play intervals are widened by ratios a pilot measured, each count's own where the pilot measured it and otherwise the largest the pilot saw; a full-play level's interval is widened by its count's difference ratio, a rule chosen here.

Glossary

Unit
The player's smallest bet. Every expected value here is in percent of a unit per round.
Spread
The range of bets from the smallest to the largest, in units. Here every count gets the same bet sizes, handed out in the order of its own estimate of the advantage.
Penetration, cut card
How far into the shoe the dealer puts the cut card, which marks where to reshuffle, in percent of the shoe.
The chart (basic strategy)
The best play for each hand with no count at all.
Fitted plays
Index tables this program chose from the data on half of the shoes and scored on the other half.
Points
Percentage points of the minimum bet per round, used when two results are compared.
Running count
The sum of the tags of every card seen so far.
True count
The running count divided by the decks left.
Balanced
A count whose tags add up to zero over a full deck.
Level
The largest tag a count uses.
Index play
A departure from the chart when the count passes a number.
Side count
A second count kept of one rank, here aces.
Insurance
The side bet on the dealer's blackjack.
Expected value
The average result per round over every way the cards can fall, written as % of a unit per round.
